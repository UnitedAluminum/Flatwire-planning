How rods, orders, spools and coils fit together

Seven worked examples · 24 August 2026 · Prepared by Nagarro for review

This page answers one question in seven pictures: when an order needs more than one rod, or a rod is shared between two orders, what comes off the line? Each example is followed from a rod arriving at the FL1 payoff through to a finished coil on a skid at FL2 — showing where spools are made, where coils are cut, and where the welds and the order boundaries land.

The figures throughout come from your own planning workbook. Nothing here is new arithmetic; it is your model, drawn out.

Weight is the quantity that survives the mill; footage is not. The same 900 lb is about 11,100 ft as it leaves FL1 and about 76,300 ft as it leaves FL2 — a sevenfold difference in cross-section. Every allocation and every split below is therefore held in pounds, and feet are worked out at the line, at the gauge actually running. This matches your own planner, which has no footage figure in it anywhere.

Before the examples: two things that are easy to misread

1. A weld joins two rods. An order boundary splits an allocation.

These are two different things and they move independently. A weld happens because a rod ran out; an order boundary happens because an allocation ran out. They can land in the same spool, in different spools, or on top of each other.

One consequence is worth stating up front, because otherwise Example 2 looks like a gap: an order fed by a single rod has no weld inside it. There is no second rod to weld to. The only welds touching such an order are the ones joining it to the neighbouring orders' rods — and those belong to the pair of rods, not to either order.

2. “Child coil” means two different things

When people say…They may meanCreated by
The coils cut from a spool 00421-C05, -C06 — these are siblings, not children. The number after C is a running count within the order. Ordinary coil completion at FL2
A split coil 00421-C05-A — a coil interrupted part-way by a change of product specification: a size or configuration change, an edge-type change, or a roll-gap change to a new target. A mid-run specification change only

A weld does not create a split coil, and neither does an order boundary. A weld adds a second source rod to a coil's certificate. An order boundary forces a cut between two coils. Neither one changes the coil's identifier.

And the coil number runs on across spools. Because C## counts within the order, an order of 40,000 lb runs C01…C45 unbroken across twenty-three spools. It does not restart at C01 for each spool. It does restart when the next order begins. CONFIRMED

Example 1 — One order, one rod, no weld

Example 1 Order 00421 · 4,000 lb · rod R00001

The simplest case the mill ever sees. One rod covers the whole order, so nothing is welded and nothing is shared.

Two full spools become four good coils. The 400 lb left on the third spool cannot.

What happens

  1. R00001 is checked in at the payoff. The operator acknowledges the pass schedule and the settings are sent to the line.
  2. FL1 winds SP-00001 to 1,800 lb. At 75 %, 90 % and 100 % of target the operator gets a weight alert. When they stop the machine, the system asks whether the stop was to remove the spool — and will not close the transaction until the operator says which carrier the next spool goes on.
  3. The same again for SP-00002.
  4. The rod runs out with only 400 lb on the third spool. It is closed short.
  5. At FL2 each full spool is cut into two 900 lb coils, and each pair goes on a skid.

The 400 lb tail is the point of this example. It is below the 800 lb customer minimum, so it makes no shippable coil. It is flagged for a supervisor — held, or offered to the customer under concession. The rule agreed is offer first, remake last, and the spool is run off either way, because FL2 has no spool stripper.

This is simple arithmetic, not a fault: 4,000 lb does not divide into 1,800 lb spools. It is the structural reason the mill welds.

What this means for you 4,000 lb consumed, 3,600 lb shipped. Every unwelded rod leaves a tail like this, and the tails add up: see Example 3.

Example 2 — One order, one rod, welded

Example 2 The case that closes rather than exists

A weld joins two rods. An order fed by one rod has no second rod — so there is no weld inside it. Here is what the welds around it actually do.

The order sits between two welds and contains neither.

Three things that look like an internal weld and are not

Looks likeWhat it actually is
The weld coming in The previous order's rod joined to R00001. The spool spanning it carries two rods and two orders — that is Example 6.
The weld going out The same, at the other end.
A coil break at FL2 Not a weld. The coil is removed and a new one starts from zero — the weight does not carry on from the break. Leftover incoming material is welded to the next coil back at FL1, or run off at FL2 and offered to the customer.

The one weld that can sit inside a single-rod order: a remake

If a weld fails its quality check, the join did not hold. The incoming rod stays staged and un-welded, the line cannot run through it, and the operator remakes the weld. Both attempts are recorded — the failure is a real quality event and hiding it would defeat the record.

Example 3 — One order, multiple rods, no weld

Example 3 Order 00421 · 8,000 lb · rods R00001 + R00002

The order needs two rods, and the line is stopped between them: rod one runs out, is checked out, rod two is checked in fresh. Nothing joins them.

Two rods, two tails. No spool contains material from both rods.

What happens

  • Two separate check-ins. Each rod gets its own run record, so the two halves of the order do not share a run.
  • No spool spans a rod. Every spool has exactly one source rod, and every coil has exactly one source rod. Certificates are single-parent throughout.
  • Two tails, 800 lb stranded. Each rod ends with 400 lb that cannot make a coil.
  • The operator may run either rod first. The plan supplies a sequence, but within an order the operator's preference is respected.
What this means for you The stranded weight scales with the rod count. Two rods strand 800 lb; eleven rods would strand 4,400 lb. Example 4 shows what welding recovers.

Example 5 — Multiple orders, multiple rods, no shared rod

Example 5 Order 00421 (8,000 lb) then order 00422 (4,000 lb)

Two orders back to back, and the boundary happens to fall exactly where a rod ends. This is the case everyone expects to be difficult, and it is the easy one.

Three rods, three tails. The order boundary costs nothing because it lines up with a rod change.

What happens — mostly, nothing special

  • The coil numbers restart. Order 00421 produces FW-00421-C01…-C08; order 00422 starts again at FW-00422-C01.
  • The rod is checked out and the payoff released before the next order begins. The second order gets a fresh check-in.
  • The two orders may therefore have different gauge, width or edge. A fresh check-in re-acknowledges the pass schedule and re-sends the settings to the line. This is only true because the boundary lines up with a rod change — see Example 6.
  • Every spool belongs to exactly one order. Every coil belongs to exactly one order.
What this means for you Multiple orders cost nothing as long as each boundary lands on a rod change. All the machinery in Example 6 exists for the one case where it does not.

Example 6 — Multiple orders, multiple rods, and a rod shared across the boundary

Example 6 Order 00421 (9,500 lb) and order 00422 (2,500 lb) sharing rod R00003

The full case. Three rods, welded continuously. R00003 is split: its first 1,500 lb finishes order 00421 and the rest starts order 00422 — without the rod ever leaving the payoff.

Two welds, and one order boundary that lines up with nothing.

The handover, step by step — the rod never comes off

  1. R00003 is checked in once, on order 00421.
  2. The system tracks consumption against 00421's 1,500 lb allocation and raises a notification the moment it is reached. It records the weight at that exact instant.
  3. The line does not stop. The material keeps running while the operator finishes what they are doing.
  4. The operator marks order 00421 complete. The system records the weight again, at that second moment. Order 00422 begins immediately, on the same rod, with no dismount and no second check-in.
Order 00421 got1,512.64 lballocated 1,500 — 12.64 lb over, run between the notification and the confirmation
Order 00422 got2,487.36 lballocated 2,500 — 12.64 lb short, because the rod ran out first

The overrun on the first order is exactly the shortfall on the second. The rod is a fixed quantity: every pound run past the notification is a pound the next order does not get. That is why the system captures the weight twice — once when the target is reached, once when you confirm — rather than discarding the difference.

And then the spool that straddles the boundary

A coil belongs to exactly one order — so this spool yields two coils that cannot ship

A finished coil carries one order number on its label and its certificate. So when a spool is wound across an order boundary, FL2 has to cut at that boundary, whatever it does to the coil weights. Here that leaves 387.36 lb on one side and 512.64 lb on the other — both below the 800 lb minimum, both unshippable, from a perfectly good 1,800 lb spool.

CLIENT INPUT REQUIRED  Can two orders on one rod have different gauge, width or edge? Because the rod is not dismounted at the boundary, there is no second check-in — and the mill settings are sent to the line at check-in. So both orders must run on the same pass schedule. If planning can put two differently-specified orders on one rod, that boundary cannot be crossed with the rod mounted: the operator would have to check the rod out and back in.

What this means for you A rod shared between two orders works, and the operator never touches the payoff. The two costs are the pound-for-pound overrun trade between the orders, and the spool that straddles the boundary. Both are avoidable by where planning places the boundary.

Example 7 — the same case, with the boundary on a coil cut

Example 7 Order 00431 (9,900 lb) and order 00432 (2,100 lb), sharing rod R00003

Example 6 again — the same three rods, the same two welds, the same spools, the same operator. The only thing that changes is where planning puts the order boundary, and it turns 900 lb of unshippable material into two coils.

As planned: 900 lb to each order, one full coil each, nothing wasted.

R00003 puts its first 1,000 lb onto SP-00005, so a 1,900 lb allocation lands the boundary 900 lb into the next spool. That is the whole trick, and it generalises to one rule:

Place an order boundary at a whole multiple of the coil weight, measured from the start of the spool the boundary will fall in. Planning can work this out, because the spool fills are known when the run is planned — it is the same inversion your own workbook already performs when it sizes FL1 spools so that FL2 can always cut valid coils.

Why 12.64 lb and not zero — and a question that follows

The split is 900 / 900 as planned. It cannot be 900 / 900 as run, because the material that keeps running between the notification and the operator's confirmation has to belong to one order or the other. In Example 6 that was 12.64 lb, and it is the same here — so the actual split is 912.64 / 887.36. The 887.36 lb coil is comfortably inside the 800–900 range; the other is 12.64 lb over the 900 lb maximum.

Assumptions behind every figure

  • The weights and counts come from your own planning workbook — 4,000 lb rods, an 1,800 lb spool target and an 800–900 lb customer coil range, alloy 1100 at 0.110″ × 0.625″ leaving FL1 and 0.0160″ × 0.625″ leaving FL2.
  • Footages are rounded to the nearest 10 ft and made to add up exactly within each spool and coil. Weight is the quantity we hold; footage is worked out at the running gauge.
  • Welding is induction and happens on the FL1 side only. FL2 cuts and re-threads rather than joining one spool to the next.
  • The 40,400 lb in Example 4 against a 40,000 lb order is your planner working as intended: the final spool is raised rather than left as a 400 lb tail that would make an unshippable coil.
